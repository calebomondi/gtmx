# Cold email: the first touch

A first email earns a reply when it is about the reader, gives them something,
and asks for one small thing. It is signed by the founder and reads like the
founder wrote it.

## Before writing
1. **Find the reason to write now.** Use the account's trigger: a funding round,
   a hire, a launch, a tender, an expansion. Name it plainly. With no trigger
   younger than about 90 days, say so to the founder rather than invent one.
2. **Pick one problem** the startup solves that this person, in this role, is
   likely to feel because of that trigger. One, not a feature list.
3. **Decide what to give.** Something useful before any ask: a finding about
   their market, a specific idea for them, a short teardown, an introduction, a
   relevant number. If the founder can't give anything real, make the offer so
   concrete that saying yes is easy.

## Writing it
- **Subject:** under 8 words, built on the hook (their name, event or number),
  no hype ("Your Nairobi expansion", "Idea for the clinic rollout").
- **First line:** about them and the trigger. Never "I hope this finds you
  well" or "My name is…".
- **Body:** the problem in their terms, the thing you're giving, and one line on
  why the startup is credible (a customer like them, a result, a number on
  file). Use only facts that are on file; never invent a customer, metric or
  quote.
- **The ask:** one low-effort question they can answer in a line ("Worth a
  15-minute look next week?", "Should I send it over?"). Not two asks.
- **Length:** 60 to 120 words, in 3 short paragraphs after the greeting. No
  attachments, no images, at most one link.
- **Sign-off:** just the founder's name. No title block needed.

## Never
- Pretend to know them, or imply a meeting or conversation that didn't happen.
- Use fake urgency ("only 2 spots left") unless it is true and on file.
- Write to someone marked do-not-contact, or to a bounced address.
- Send. Every email waits for the founder's click.
